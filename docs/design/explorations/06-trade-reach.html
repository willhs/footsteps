What it shows

On the map: from the clicked place, thin neutral arcs run to where its things came from. Solid, cyan-tinted arcs are objects chemically traced or found and recorded; dashed grey arcs are inferred. Thicker means stronger evidence. Each far end carries the good and its distance. The arcs say "from here to there", not the route: most goods moved hand to hand over many steps.

In the details panel: "What came from far away": each good with its source, distance, how it moved (hand to hand, boat, caravan, ship) and how we know (chemically sourced, found as an object, written record, inferred). Seshat's markets and money for the place where it has them. One imagined line, "A trader's journey", behind the warm button.

Over time: one line, log scale: the longest well-evidenced distance goods moved. Tens of km for obsidian in the Middle Stone Age, ~1,000+ km for Spondylus shell, thousands for Bronze Age tin, then ~20,000 km, half way round the Earth, the most a straight line can be.

Kind

  • Measured: objects traced to a source by chemistry (obsidian, tin, silver, Spondylus, Alpine jade), finds of foreign objects (Roman pottery at Arikamedu, Indian glass beads in the Limpopo valley), written records (port books, customs ledgers, Tomé Pires on Melaka).
  • Modelled: inferred links (a good that must have come from somewhere far, with no sourced object yet), evidence strength, and the "how it moved" guess for early periods.
  • Imagined: a trader's journey, written from the place's goods and labelled speculative.

Verdict: can get it for showcase places. There is no global dataset, but a few dozen hand-curated places with sourced objects tell the story well. Everywhere else the panel should say "no curated routes here yet" rather than guess.

Risks

  • Curation bias. Archaeology and records are densest for Europe, the Near East and China. Pick showcases on purpose from Africa, South and Southeast Asia, the Americas and the Pacific (as the slider's places do).
  • Arcs imply fixed routes and single journeys. Most goods moved hand to hand, village to village. Label arcs "from–to, not the route", and make "hand to hand" the default way it moved for early periods.
  • Tribute and plunder look like trade. A foreign object can arrive as a gift, tribute or loot. Where the record says so, the "how it moved" field says so; otherwise "exchange (kind unknown)".
  • Textbook drift. A grand Silk Road map is exactly what the vision rejects. Keep it to one clicked place.

Gender and war

Gender: leave out by default. Add only well-sourced cases as a line in the panel, for example West African market women in the trade records, and never infer who traded from the way of life.

War: plunder, tribute and enslaved people moved along the same paths. My call: do not hide the slave trade. From 1500 to the 1860s, where a place was a point of capture or sale, the panel says so plainly and the map draws people forced to move as a separate warm dotted line (they are people, so they get the people colour, never a goods line), with SlaveVoyages counts. Plunder and tribute are labelled as such in "how it moved", never drawn as trade.
